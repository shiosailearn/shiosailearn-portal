Where Do High School Dropouts Go: Two Alternative Routes, South Africa and Japan

kounin-pass

South Africa's 2025 matric exam hit a record-high 88% pass rate.

The headlines were full of "highest ever." The education minister gave a speech. The number spread across social media. But behind the number is a cold reality.

Around 40% of Grade 8 students disappear before they ever reach the matric exam. That 88% is the pass rate "among those who sat the test." According to the DA, the real pass rate across the full cohort sits at 51–55%. Of the 1.2 million students in Grade 8 in 2014, only 778,000 actually sat the matric in 2019.

Japan's High School Equivalency Exam (kounin, 高卒認定) is for people who couldn't earn credits in high school — so the numbers are smaller. Around 17,000 people sit it per year (2025 figures). Overall pass rate for clearing every subject at once is about 47%, though the system lets you carry subjects over, so the real picture is different. Average age of those who pass: 22.3. It's not just high school dropouts — many people come back to it after entering the workforce. Oldest on record: 86.

Let's put the two side by side.

South Africa (NSC matric) Japan (kounin)
Exam sessions Once a year (reruns available) Twice a year (August, November)
Official pass rate 88% (of those who sat) 47% (all subjects at once)
Real cohort rate ~51–55% Very small fraction (~7,900/year pass fully)
What the cert gets you Direct university entry and employment — treated as finishing school University entry only, plus eligibility for some qualifications. Does not count as graduating high school
Second chances Second Chance Programme / Adult Matric (resit support) Subjects carry over between sessions

A matric certificate in South Africa opens doors directly to enter University, or employment. There are three pass tiers, but all of them mean you've "finished school" in the eyes of society: Specifically, Companies, government agencies. If you have an NSC, they treat you the same as a high school graduate.

Japan's kounin is different. The Ministry of Education's official position: passing does not change your highest level of education. Even with kounin, you can't write "high school graduate" on a job application unless you actually graduated high school. You only become a "university graduate" once you get into university.

Employer awareness of kounin sits at around 60% (MEXT, 2013 survey). And knowing it exists is one thing — whether they treat holders the same as high school graduates in hiring is another. Looking at the surveys, there's a significant gap between recognition and actual practice.

Why does this gap exist? Because the two systems were built for different purposes.

Matric is designed as a threshold. It sits at the endpoint of 12 years of compulsory schooling, a social clearance document. That can't be separated from South Africa's post-apartheid history of trying to close the education gap.

Japan's kounin, by contrast, started as the University Entrance Qualification Exam (daiken, 大検). The name changed in 2004, and it became easier to sit, but the underlying logic didn't change: a stepping stone to university. The credential itself was never meant to be a destination. In other words, the "high school equivalency" is really just a means to an end — and the end is university.

This difference shows up as a message to anyone who dropped out.

South Africa: "Come back. Sit the exam. Get matric. You can go to university, you can get a job."

Japan: "Kounin is a certificate of 'high school equivalency'. Basically, it's a tool for university entry. If you don't go to university, you're still effectively a middle school leaver."

That said: given South Africa's high unemployment rate, getting a job after matric is a hard fight. Passing matric doesn't automatically mean you can get work. But if Japan's kounin also means you're effectively middle-school-educated without university, then the message is essentially "what you learned is only useful for university entrance." Learning should have more diverse value than that.

Looking at who sits kounin in Japan: 57.5% are high school dropouts, 14.5% are currently enrolled in full-time high school, 9.8% never made it past middle school (2023 data). The majority are people who tried the conventional route and left partway through.

South Africa sees its biggest dropout spike between Grades 10 and 11. Poverty, family circumstances, pregnancy, farm labor during harvest. The reasons overlap with Japan's in some ways and diverge sharply in others.

What both share: a gap between what pass rate statistics show and how many people actually make it through the system. The numbers the system tracks and the reality experienced by people who dropped out are telling different stories.

What makes Kounin Pass (高認パス) interesting is that it tries to address this gap directly. A tool that analyzes past papers and shows the shortest path to passing even if it doesn't fix the structural problem. But it makes the entrance to the system a little less daunting to find.

South Africa's Second Chance Programme is working the same angle. Resit support, study materials, online classes. "Don't change the system. Just get more people back through the door."

Neither is perfect. South Africa leaves in place a structure where pass rates are easy to manipulate politically. Japan operates with a fundamental contradiction but passing doesn't give you the credential.

Still, looking at the two side by side makes one thing clear. Having an alternative route and having an alternative route that actually works in society are completely different problems. The second one is much harder.

Will Japan's kounin ever reach the point where "having this means you're treated the same as a high school grad"? Will South Africa ever officially adopt the cohort pass rate as a primary metric?

Neither looks likely anytime soon. But there's no reason either is impossible.
